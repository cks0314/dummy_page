Navigation Environment

Figure 1. Navigation environment considered in this work. (a) Bicycle-model robot with state and control inputs. (b) Relative robot-obstacle geometry used throughout the paper.

We consider a bicycle-model robot navigating toward a goal in an environment populated with independently maneuvering unicycle obstacles. Unlike constant-velocity obstacles, these agents may continuously change their speed and heading, producing time-varying relative motion with the robot. The robot must generate collision-free trajectories while respecting its nonholonomic dynamics and continuously reasoning about the relative position and velocity of nearby obstacles in real time.


The Problem

Conventional Dynamic Parabolic Control Barrier Functions (DPCBFs) guarantee safety only when surrounding obstacles maintain constant velocity. In realistic environments, obstacles may accelerate, decelerate, or change their heading, introducing dynamics that invalidate the nominal safety certificate.

Failure of conventional DPCBF. Although the nominal barrier constraint is satisfied, an obstacle begins maneuvering, causing the underlying safety guarantee to become invalid.

Why does the guarantee fail?

Conventional DPCBF assumes that the obstacle motion model perfectly matches the true dynamics. Once an obstacle starts maneuvering, the actual barrier evolution differs from the nominal prediction by an additional disturbance term.

$$ \dot h = \dot h_{\rm DPCBF} $$
$$ \dot h_{\rm true} = \dot h_{\rm DPCBF} + \color{red}{\Delta(t)} $$

where \( \Delta(t) \) captures the obstacle's unknown maneuvering behavior arising from acceleration and turning-rate effects. Because this term is ignored, the nominal barrier certificate can become invalid even though the optimization problem remains feasible.

$$ \Delta(t)= a_{\rm obs}\cos\tilde{\theta}_{\rm obs} + v_{\rm obs}\omega_{\rm obs} \left( 2\lambda \dot{\tilde y}_{\rm rel} \cos\tilde{\theta}_{\rm obs} - \sin\tilde{\theta}_{\rm obs} \right) $$

The key question is therefore:

Can we recover provable safety guarantees when surrounding obstacles are allowed to maneuver within known physical limits?

Proposed Algorithm: AR-DPCBF

Instead of assuming obstacles move with constant velocity, AR-DPCBF models obstacle maneuverability explicitly and analytically reserves the safety margin required to remain collision-free under worst-case bounded maneuvers.

The fundamental limitation of conventional DPCBF is that it certifies safety only for the nominal obstacle motion model. Once an obstacle accelerates or changes its heading, the true barrier dynamics deviate from the nominal prediction, invalidating the safety certificate.

AR-DPCBF addresses this limitation by treating each obstacle as a bounded adversary whose acceleration and turning rate are constrained by known physical capability limits. Rather than reacting after the maneuver occurs, the controller explicitly accounts for the worst-case admissible maneuver during barrier construction.

The animation above illustrates this principle geometrically. As the maneuverability bound $\kappa$ increases, the controller analytically reserves a larger fraction of the available safety margin by contracting both the physical unsafe set and the admissible relative-velocity boundary. Larger maneuvering capability therefore results in a more conservative—but provably valid—safety certificate.

Importantly, this contraction is not a heuristic safety buffer. The contracted barrier parameters are derived directly from the obstacle capability bounds, yielding the minimum contraction required to guarantee safety under all admissible maneuvers while avoiding unnecessary conservatism.

Bounded Adversary

Obstacles are modeled as bounded adversaries whose acceleration and turning rate are limited by known physical capabilities instead of assuming constant velocity.

Analytical Safety Margin

The barrier contracts analytically as maneuverability increases, reserving exactly the safety margin required to compensate for worst-case obstacle maneuvers.

Provable Robustness

The resulting safety certificate remains valid under every obstacle maneuver that satisfies the assumed capability bounds, recovering formal guarantees that conventional DPCBF cannot provide.

Quantitative Performance

The qualitative improvements are consistently reflected across randomized environments with varying adversarial populations and maneuvering capabilities.

Increasing the number of adversarial obstacles substantially degrades conventional DPCBF, whereas the proposed formulations consistently reduce both barrier violations and collisions.

Buffer Soft AR-DPCBF maintains the lowest barrier violation as obstacle capability increases while requiring substantially lower optimization cost than the hard formulation.

Abstract

Adversarial-Robust Dynamic Parabolic Control Barrier Functions (AR-DPCBF) extend Dynamic Parabolic Control Barrier Functions (DPCBFs) to dynamic environments with maneuvering obstacles. Unlike DPCBF, which assumes constant obstacle velocity, AR-DPCBF models obstacle maneuvers through a bounded-adversary framework and derives a geometry-preserving robust safety certificate with formal guarantees.

The proposed approach introduces Adversarial Control Barrier Functions (A-CBFs), closed-form parameter contractions, explicit feasibility conditions, and an online obstacle capability estimator. Two soft-constrained variants further improve feasibility in cluttered environments while retaining nominal DPCBF safety.

Extensive simulations demonstrate significant reductions in barrier violations and collisions compared with DPCBF, with Buffer Soft AR-DPCBF providing the best overall trade-off between safety, feasibility, and robustness.
